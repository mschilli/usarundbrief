@Z: Vacationing in Florida

<pic images/stgeorge-beach.ps Embarrassing Florida tourists on the beach>

<em>Angelika</em>
Attentive Rundbrief readers know that we are die-hard Hawaii fans and take
every opportunity to visit those beautiful islands. Nevertheless, you have to
remain flexible and explore other regions as well. We had long wanted to return
to Florida, since it had been decades since we had been there—on our first trip
to the United States, in fact. Back then we explored southern Florida separately
and visited Key West, among other places.

<pic images/florida-map.ps The Florida Panhandle is at the northwestern tip.>
<pic images/stgeorge-gated.ps An empty beach on St. George; houses in a "gated community" on the right>

This time we ended up at the opposite end of the state, at its northwesternmost
tip, on the so-called "Forgotten Coast." This stretch of coastline is part of
the Florida Panhandle and lies between the state capital, Tallahassee, and
Panama City. As the name suggests, it is somewhat off the beaten path, and life
there is quiet and leisurely. Tourists come to enjoy the long, beautiful sandy
beaches, swim in the Gulf of Mexico, collect seashells, and eat delicious fish.
The father of a former student had told me about this area and strongly
recommended that I visit it.

<pic images/eastpoint-house.ps Our superb vacation home in Eastpoint.>
<HTMLONLY> <pic images/eastpoint-stilts.ps Vacation homes rebuilt after the 2018 hurricane.> </HTMLONLY>

As for the climate, Florida is hot and humid during the summer months, and
<LINK>https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Michael|hurricanes</LINK> regularly
blow through as well. Spring is therefore an ideal time to visit the state and
avoid all that. My Easter break was perfect timing. No sooner said than done:
we flew into Tampa and then drove our rental car up to the small fishing
village of Eastpoint, where we had rented a little house. It had a gorgeous
porch with rocking chairs and was painted entirely turquoise. Just looking at
it put us in a good mood.

<pic images/eastpoint-gecko.ps A friendly gecko in the house keeps the insects under control.>

Eastpoint is a typical small-town American community right on Apalachicola Bay.
It consists mainly of a main road, a rustic supermarket with the charming name
"Big Top," a few restaurants and shops, and plenty of fishing boats, since the
area traditionally makes its living from fishing.

<pic images/apalachi-sign.ps Funny town names in the Florida Panhandle>

Surprisingly, the town even has a small brewery, the Eastpoint Beer Company,
with an attached pub overlooking the bay where you can eat good pizza and drink
excellent beer at unbeatable prices. Coming from California, we rubbed our
eyes in amazement when the bill arrived.

<pic images/eastpoint-market.ps The rustic supermarket in Eastpoint.>
<pic images/deals-bill.ps Eating out is considerably cheaper in Florida than in California.>

Directly across from Eastpoint, connected by a bridge, is St. George Island,
one of the area's many barrier islands. The island, around 22 miles (35
kilometers) long, lies in the Gulf of Mexico and is home to Dr. Julian G. Bruce
St. George Island State Park at its eastern end.

The island has wonderfully long beaches of powdery white sand that sparkles so
brightly in the sun it practically blinds you. Every time we crossed the
bridge, pelicans accompanied us, enjoying the updraft and gliding alongside the
bridge. A magnificent spectacle.

<HTMLONLY><pic images/stjoseph-pelicans.ps Pelicans fly around everywhere.></HTMLONLY>

On our very first day, we hiked to the tip of the island, a mere 15 kilometers
there and back. Our tender little feet were rather sore afterward, but we were
rewarded with fantastic views and had the trail almost entirely to ourselves.
To cool off, we went for a swim in the Gulf at the end of the hike.

<HTMLONLY><pic images/stgeorge-parking.ps One car is not like the others.></HTMLONLY>

What kept surprising us was that we could always find a parking space right by
the most beautiful beaches, and it did not even cost anything. Only the state
park charged admission ($6 per car). The people in this area were extremely
friendly and already spoke with a pronounced Southern accent.
Before traveling to Florida, we were repeatedly asked how anyone could visit
such a reactionary state. The prejudices between California and Florida seem
to be mutual. What a shame!

<pic images/stjoseph-fishermen.ps Recreational anglers vacationing in Florida.>

To finish the trip, we visited a dear old friend who has lived in Tampa for
many years because she could no longer afford California's high cost of living.
Not only are groceries cheaper in Florida, but rent and electricity also cost
a fraction of what they do in California. And an acquaintance who lives in a
gated community in Tampa told us that she paid only $350,000 for her spacious
bungalow!

<pic images/sunpass-toll.ps Traveling Florida's toll roads with SunPass.>

Florida does charge fees for using toll roads, however. We stuck a device
called a "SunPass" to the windshield of the rental car and set up an account
online. If you do not do that, the rental-car company charges a basic fee for
every day a toll-road camera photographs the car. The little sticker, which
you can buy on Amazon, saves you that expense. It works as a transponder, and
small amounts are deducted whenever you use a toll road. Over two weeks,
the total came to only about ten dollars.

@Z: The Piggly Wiggly Grocery Chain

<pic images/piggly-wiggly-point.ps The Rundbrief reporter tests the Piggly Wiggly supermarket chain>

<em>Michael</em> When you travel within America, you often find the same retail
chains even three thousand kilometers from home. Every Starbucks or McDonald's
looks much the same inside, whether it is in California or Florida, so the
customer does not have to adjust much.

Things get interesting with grocery stores, because California and the entire
West Coast have no Aldi, for example, since Trader Joe's represents the Albrecht
conglomerate there (<RBLINK>20050925#3</RBLINK>). Outside the Bay Area but still
in California, branches of Vons or Ralph's are common. Things get really funny
on the East Coast, however, where you find not only actual Aldi stores but also
supermarkets with the hilarious name Piggly Wiggly.

<pic images/piggly-wiggly-cooler.ps The reporters also purchased this cooler bag with a Piggly Wiggly logo>

Hardly anyone knows that Piggly Wiggly invented the self-service supermarket.
It was not all that long ago that a customer in a corner grocery store had to
say, "One can of beans, please," prompting the clerk behind the counter to set
off in search of one in the storeroom. Piggly Wiggly invented the revolutionary
idea of putting a shopping basket in the customer's hand and letting that
customer walk through the aisles unassisted, taking the desired goods from the
shelves without interacting with store employees at all. It boggles the mind!

<pic images/piggly-wiggly-bags.ps The bagging madness common in rural America.>

Today, Piggly Wiggly is a well-stocked supermarket with no major differences
from other chains. At the checkout of the branch we visited, however, we
encountered the bagging madness common in rural America. Behind the cashier is
a carousel with about eight compartments, each holding one open bag and dozens
of additional folded bags. After scanning the groceries, the cashier
distributes them among the open bags and rotates the carousel each time. The
open bags are by no means filled up; instead, the groceries are spread around
liberally—one loaf of bread in one bag, one bottle of juice in the next, and so
on—until you finally push a cart containing 20 bags out to your car. The bags
are also of the worst conceivable quality. They are so thin that you cannot
even use them to take out the trash later without disaster striking.

<pic images/piggly-wiggly-hat.ps The new cap looks excellent on Angelika.>

Angelika normally brings her own bags to put a stop to this and prowls around
the conveyor belt like a panther ready to pounce as soon as anyone so much as
moves toward a plastic bag. In this case, though, she did not dare throw the
cashier off her rhythm. We might have attracted unwelcome attention! As
Californians, we have to be careful: residents of other states think
Californians are crazy. But that is not fair—I personally cannot do anything
about the absurd politics in our state!

@Z: Yet More Elections in California

<pic images/steyer-flyer.ps Private businessman Tom Steyer wants to become governor.>

<em>Angelika</em>
The so-called midterms, which will take place this November, are already on
everyone's lips. These are the important midterm elections in which many seats
in the House of Representatives and the Senate are up for election.
Traditionally, the party of the incumbent president loses a number of seats in
such elections. This time, 35 of the 100 Senate seats are up for election, as
are all 435 seats in the House of Representatives.

Who will enter the race in November is determined by the primary elections.
Every state holds these so-called primaries. However, the primaries do not take
place on the same day in every state; instead, each state has its own schedule.
In California, the primaries already took place in June. For most major
offices, California uses a system known as a "Top-Two Open Primary," which,
amusingly, is also often referred to as a "jungle primary." California uses a
different system only for presidential elections.

<pic images/election-gov-primary.ps Governor primary election results: Only the top two candidates advance.>

So what exactly is behind this "top-two system"? The name gives some of it
away. For the gubernatorial election, seats in the US Congress, and elections
to the California State Legislature, all candidates appear on a single ballot,
regardless of their party affiliation. Every registered voter can vote for any
candidate on that ballot, regardless of the party preference they stated when
registering to vote. The two candidates who receive the most votes then advance
to the general election in November, again regardless of which party they
belong to.

<pic images/election-saikat.ps This gentleman wants to become a member of Congress.>

Things are often different in other states. In so-called closed primaries,
voters can generally vote only for candidates from the party whose preference
they stated when registering. Someone registered as a Democrat, for example,
can then participate only in the Democratic primary. The rules do vary from
state to state, however. Voters can generally change their party preference
again as well. I know people who deliberately register with the opposing party
in such states because they want to influence that party's choice of
candidates.

In principle, this primary system seems very democratic, since it is ultimately
the voters who decide which candidates will run in the actual election. In
reality, however, turnout in primaries is usually significantly lower than in
general elections. As a result, it is often only the party base that goes to
the polls, and the candidates selected are more extreme than those the average
member of the population actually wants. Well, elections have consequences,
and not showing up is a very bad idea. Sure enough, voter turnout in California
was below 35% of eligible voters this time. That was already higher than usual,
and voters selected not only the candidates for the US Congress but also those
for the <LINK>https://www.ppic.org/blog/how-election-reform-played-out-in-californias-june-primary/|office of governor of California</LINK>.
More on that in a moment.

<pic images/wiener-noe.ps House candidate Scott Wiener in Noe Valley.>

But back to California's primary system. Only the two candidates with the most
votes advance. A candidate therefore does not need a majority, but merely has
to finish in the top two. This can result in two candidates from the same party
winning and advancing to the general election. That very nearly happened in
California's primaries this time. Another curiosity of this system, incidentally,
is that even if a candidate receives more than 50 percent of the vote in the
primary, the top two finishers still face each other, regardless of how large
the winner's lead was in the primary.

<pic images/election-wiener-sign.ps This resident is campaigning for congressional candidate Scott Wiener.>

Our governor, Gavin Newsom, cannot run again after two terms, so Californians
had to decide whom to send into the race to succeed him. California, of course,
is known as a very progressive state in which the Democratic Party is strongly
dominant. It would therefore seem logical to assume that two Democrats would
advance to the final round. Far from it. This year, a whole host of Democratic
candidates had entered the race. As a result, Democratic votes were split
among many candidates. For a time, it actually appeared that two Republicans
might make it into the "top two." After all, when votes are spread among many
candidates, the percentage received by each individual candidate naturally
falls.

<pic images/election-ballot.ps (#4) 50 candidates for governor>

In the end, however, things turned out differently. A Democrat and a
Republican are now facing each other: Xavier Becerra versus Steve Hilton.
Becerra is an experienced career politician who has served, among other roles,
as California attorney general and US secretary of health and human services.
Hilton, by contrast, has never held political office. He did, however, work as
a political adviser in the United Kingdom and later became known as a
television host.

Despite the sheer abundance of 50 candidates (figure (#4)), voters somehow
still faced a rather limited choice. There was no truly charismatic standard-
bearer for California—someone who might arrive with fresh ideas for this highly
complex and complicated state.

<pic images/wiener-flyer.ps Scott Wiener wants to be elected to the House of Representatives.>

It remains to be seen what will actually happen in California in November. As
already mentioned, California is considered one of the safest Democratic
strongholds in the United States, and a Republican governor would be quite a
surprise at present. Nevertheless, many Californians are decidedly dissatisfied
with the policies of recent years, particularly because of the high cost of
living, the housing shortage, homelessness, high energy prices, and the still-
unresolved infrastructure problems. Although the latest polls show Becerra
well ahead, November is still a long way off in political terms.

In the simultaneously held primary for the House seat being vacated by the
prominent representative Nancy Pelosi, local politicians Scott Wiener and
Connie Chan qualified for the general election. Scott Wiener currently serves
in the California State Senate, while Connie Chan serves as a supervisor on
the San Francisco Board of Supervisors. The two candidates are currently
engaged in a media mudslinging match, and a few days ago, following complaints
from within his own party, Scott Wiener had to take down the AI website
<LINK>https://sfist.com/2026/08/04/scott-wiener-campaign-launches-ai-chatbot|conniechan.ai</LINK>. The site portrayed the candidate giving fictional and supposedly
funny answers to current political questions in a Chinese accent. As always, the
campaign is merciless, while discussion of substantive issues is more the
exception than the rule.

@Z: Voting in Person

<pic images/election-polling-sidewalk.ps Michael marks his candidates at a polling place in a garage.>

<em>Michael</em>
As everyone knows, our elections are always held on Tuesdays so people can dash
from work to the polls. And polling places are by no means limited to schools
or public buildings. Private citizens are also paid to clear out their garages,
fill them with folding tables and voting booths, and provide staff so voters
can come and cast their ballots there.

<pic images/election-i-voted.ps (#5) Michael has the official voting sticker.>

Here in California, most voters cast their ballots by mail. If you are
registered accordingly, the election materials arrive at your home weeks
before the election. You mark your candidates and put the ballots into the
enclosed envelope. You have to date and sign it and also write your address on
it. Then you put the sealed envelope into the nearest mailbox or one of the
official ballot boxes distributed across the city.

<pic images/election-pp-map.ps The map shows the nearest polling place.>

I had never been inside a polling place and had always voted by mail. But since
my motto these days is to put every silly idea into practice immediately, I
cycled to the nearest polling place on Election Day: the garage of a detached
house about 500 meters from our apartment. California famously performs no ID
checks during voting, so I entered the garage and merely gave my name to two
girls of about twelve who were working as receptionists at a folding
table. They began searching for me in a roughly 100-page paper directory with
perhaps 20 names per page.

<pic images/election-polling-place.ps Michael follows the signs to the polling place.>

But alas, they could not find me! No problem, said another election worker—an
adult this time. I merely had to fill out a form with my name, address, date of
birth, and party preference (Republican or Democrat), and then I would receive
a provisional ballot. I read the form and thought: This cannot be right; I
have been registered to vote for years. I told another election worker that I
had again received ballots in the mail but wanted to vote in the garage just
for the adventure of it. She knew that anyone who already had the ballots at
home would not appear on the paper voter roll. I could go and fetch my ballots,
though.

<pic images/election-booth.ps Michael marks his favorites in the voting booth.>

At that point I was the only voter in the garage, and the election workers
surely thought I was not quite right in the head, but I did not care. After
all, I was on assignment as a Rundbrief reporter, and our dear little readers
demand hard-hitting stories researched in the thick of real life! So I rode my
bicycle home again and fetched the papers. When I returned to the garage, no
other voter had appeared, and the election workers explained that they could
either give me new ballots to fill out after taking mine away, or I could fill
out my own papers, put them into the envelope, sign it, and place it in their
ballot box.

<pic images/election-portal.ps (#7) You can see online whether your vote has already been counted.>

That is what I did, and the twelve-year-old girls handed me an "I Voted"
sticker (Figure (#5)). I kept the detachable ballot stubs. Days later, I was
able to follow online from home as my vote was counted. On
<LINK>https://sfelections.org/tools/portal/vbmstatus.php|sfelections.org</LINK>,
I had to enter our house number, my date of birth, and our ZIP code, and then I
could see how far my ballots had progressed through the process (Figure (#7)).

It took a good week for the status to switch from "Envelope opened" to "Vote
counted," and apparently my vote was properly included in the election.
Hooray!

@Z: Sea Creatures Washed Ashore

<pic images/btw-sailor-shoes.ps The hiker finds a by-the-wind sailor on the beach.>

<em>Michael</em> Most people think of San Francisco as a crazy big city and
forget that the entire western side lies directly on the Pacific, whose mighty
waves pound our local beach. We often walk along the beach; one of our favorite
routes is 10 kilometers long!

<pic images/ob-glass.ps No creatures, just pieces of wave-polished glass washed ashore.>

Along the way, we find all kinds of sea creatures washed ashore. Seashells, of
course; the iconic sand dollars; great clumps of kelp; giant crabs; we have
even seen a whale skeleton (Figure (#6))! And then, of course, there are
smoothly polished shards of glass and stones in every color and shape. We
usually take a few finds home for our extensive collection.

<pic images/btw-sailor-group.ps (#2) The little jellyfish-like sailors wash ashore in southwesterly winds.>

Every now and then, we encounter a new creature. Recently, for example, we came
across thousands of tiny jellyfish with raised, translucent, gelatinous sails
(Figure (#2)). They are about the size of a chicken egg, shimmer an exotic
blue, and are called <LINK>https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velella|by-the-wind sailors</LINK>,
or "Velella velella" in scientific Latin.

<pic images/ob-whale.ps (#6) The skeleton of a whale washed ashore near Fort Funston.>

The sail, which helps them zip around the ocean like tiny windsurfers, looks as
though it is made of plastic and contains circular ribs that make it resemble
a target. They let the prevailing northwesterly winds propel them, but when the
wind turns southwest—as it usually does here in Northern California in the
spring—it blows them onto the Pacific beaches in huge numbers, where walkers
marvel at them as though they were the seventh wonder of the world.

<pic images/ob-kelp.ps Kelp washed ashore at Ocean Beach>

The book
<LINK>https://www.amazon.com/Ocean-Beach-Fog-Fauna-Flora/dp/1597146943|Ocean
Beach by Eddy Rubin</LINK> contains numerous details about sea creatures washed
ashore—seemingly more than Herman Melville's "Moby-Dick" contains about the
different species of whales.

<pic images/ob-eddy-rubin.ps The highly readable book "Ocean Beach" by Eddy Rubin>

According to the book, the by-the-wind sailor's scientific Latin name is
"Velella velella." Author and surfing legend Eddy Rubin knows San Francisco's
Ocean Beach better than anyone. For decades, he has not only ridden its
challenging waves but also analyzed and explained the flora and fauna of our
local beach in minute detail. Well worth reading!

@Z: Birthright Citizenship

<pic images/amendments.ps The 14th Amendment to the Constitution guarantees birthright citizenship.>

<em>Angelika</em>
One of the interesting rights in the United States is that, as a general rule,
anyone born on American soil (jus soli) automatically acquires U.S.
citizenship. It does not matter what citizenship the parents hold or what their
immigration status is. The 14th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution forms the
basis for this rule. There are, however, a few exceptions, including children
of foreign diplomats and certain hostile foreign forces, whatever exactly that
may mean.

This rule has long been a thorn in our president's side. One of his first
official acts in January 2025 was therefore to issue an executive order
restricting automatic citizenship for children of parents without legal
immigration status, as well as for children of parents with certain temporary
visas, such as students, tourists, or people with temporary work visas. The
executive order was immediately challenged in court and ultimately ended up
before the U.S. Supreme Court. Many legal experts considered the measure highly
questionable on constitutional grounds, since the 14th Amendment has protected
birthright citizenship for more than 150 years. And indeed, on June 30, 2026,
the U.S. Supreme Court struck down the administration's order by a vote of 6
to 3. The Court thus ruled that children of immigrants without legal status and
of parents with temporary visas are generally protected by birthright
citizenship.

That, however, was far from the end of the matter. As early as August 6, 2026,
our president signed two new executive orders. One attempts to interpret more
broadly the existing exceptions for children of foreign diplomats and
so-called "alien enemies." The other order is directed in particular against
so-called birth tourism and against cases in which foreign nationals enter the
United States on a visa specifically to give birth to a child there so that the
child will acquire U.S. citizenship. These new rules are also likely to be
challenged in court.

It is difficult to determine how many children are actually born in the United
States as a result of birth tourism. There are no official statistics, so the
figures vary considerably depending on how they are calculated. For 2024, the
CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) recorded 
<LINK>https://ftp.cdc.gov/pub/Health_Statistics/NCHS/Dataset_Documentation/DVS/natality/UserGuide2024.pdf|a total of 9,576</LINK>
births to women who reported a foreign residential address.
This figure, however, is unlikely to consist exclusively of genuine cases of
birth tourism. A much broader estimate by the
<LINK>https://www.migrationpolicy.org/journal/policy-beat/though-rare-birth-tourism-united-states-sparks-outsized-concern|Migration Policy Institute</LINK>
puts
the number at 20,000 to 26,000 births per year.
Even this higher figure represents less than one percent of the more than 3.5
million births in the United States each year.

What I actually wanted to talk about in this context, though, is the role San
Francisco played long ago in the interpretation of the 14th Amendment. The
story concerns Wong Kim Ark, who was born in San Francisco's Chinatown in 1873
to Chinese immigrant parents. When Wong Kim Ark returned to San Francisco
after an extended trip to China, he was denied entry on the grounds that he was
supposedly not a U.S. citizen. Upon arrival, he was told that the 14th Amendment
to the Constitution did not apply to him. He refused to accept this and took
his case to the Supreme Court. In March 1898, the Court affirmed that children
born in the United States to foreign parents generally acquire U.S.
citizenship as well.

Amusingly, the subject also came up during this year's World Cup. Soccer player
Folarin Balogun, who played for the U.S. team and received a red card that was
later rescinded after a controversial phone call from Trump, was able to play
for the U.S. team only because he acquired American citizenship by being born
on American soil. His mother was seven months pregnant when she visited New
York. Because of how far along she was in her pregnancy, the airline would not
fly her back to London, so she ultimately gave birth to her son in New York.
Quite the story!

@Z: Bank Transfers in America

<pic images/wire-transfer-diagram.ps How do you transfer money in America?>

<em>Michael</em>
For almost 30 years now, we have paid our rent every month with a check. We put
it into a stamped envelope addressed to the landlord and drop it into a
mailbox. About a week later, we see on the bank's website that the check has
been cashed and the money has left the account. You might think this could be
handled more easily by direct debit, but far from it: bank transfers and direct
debits are still surprisingly complicated in America.

<pic images/bank-wire-detail.ps Behind the scenes, things get complicated.>

First of all, most transactions are handled with credit cards. While Amazon
Germany happily charges purchases directly to the buyer's bank account, Amazon
USA charges the buyer's credit card, which in turn collects the money from the
customer at the end of the billing period. The costs incurred by the merchant
are substantial: the credit-card company takes between 3 and 5% of the invoice
amount. The benefit to the store is that it gets its money within a few days,
while the card company absorbs any fraud.

In recent years, more and more government agencies have realized that these
card fees are outrageously high. Besides, the DMV and the IRS know their
customers, and it is relatively unlikely that someone would use a stolen card
to commit fraud when paying vehicle-registration fees: it would come to light
immediately, and the government agency would have the culprit in its grasp.

Consequently, the DMV and IRS have charged extra fees for many years if you pay
vehicle-registration fees or a tax debt by credit card. As an alternative,
they offer to withdraw the money from the payer's account when the payer
provides an account number and routing number. The government agency then
initiates what is known as a debit, a transaction not unlike a German direct
debit. The money is withdrawn from the payer's account and credited to the
agency.

But if, for example, a tenant wants to pay rent by bank transfer, the fees are
eye-opening: banks charge exorbitant amounts for a wire transfer from a
checking account to a private individual, typically around $20. Institutions
sometimes waive the fee for customers with extremely large accounts. Banks
also allow companies to set up regular transfers known as "direct deposits,"
for example to pay salaries. When a private individual opens a bank account,
the account is usually free if the customer establishes one or more direct
deposits. These can include not only wages from an employer but also regular
government payments such as Social Security benefits. That ties the customer
to the bank and keeps away ne'er-do-wells without a regular income.

<pic images/bank-check.ps A "personal check" from a checkbook for private payments>

In private life, nobody simply transfers money to another account. Instead,
service providers such as Venmo or Zelle step into the breach. When a small
contractor repairs something at the house, he usually wants to be paid through
Venmo. The payment then reaches the recipient immediately, securely, and
irreversibly, while Venmo bears the risk when it subsequently withdraws the
amount from the payer's bank account.

<pic images/mailbox-stolen-checks.ps (#1) A mailbox with a warning taped to it.>

In the past, payments to private individuals were made by check. A father gave
his college-age son a check. If you had no cash with you and wanted to pay your
share of lunch, you gave the other person a check. Rent, too, was—and still
is—paid by check, either by sliding the rent check under the landlord's or
property manager's door at the beginning of the month, or by sending it through
the mail.

What happens if the check is stolen? That is not as unlikely as it sounds.
Recently I noticed that someone had posted a note on a mailbox—ironically, the
one outside the Noe Valley post office—after checks he had deposited there were
stolen (Figure (#1)).

In such cases, you might expect the bank to verify that everything is in order
when someone tries to cash a stolen check. It might check, for example, whether
the person cashing it is the payee named on the check, or whether the signature
with which the recipient endorsed the back matches the signature on file for
the recipient's account. Wrong—the bank checks nothing at all!

<pic images/atm.ps Hardly anyone uses good old cash anymore.>

Not even for checks worth several thousand dollars. The bank simply cashes
everything without scrutiny and acts only if the person who wrote the check
complains. That does not happen until the intended recipient contacts the
sender to complain that the check has not arrived, whereupon the sender looks
and discovers that it was indeed cashed. After some back-and-forth, the bank
then has to reverse the transaction, usually swallowing the loss itself, and
the sender writes a new check and sends it to the recipient—hopefully with
better luck this time.

The latest trick is known as check "bleaching." The criminal removes certain
fields from the check and inserts a new payee and amount. Social media is full
of stories about people who have been cheated out of thousands of dollars this
way. Anyone who does not watch the activity in their account like a hawk risks
major losses that can be recovered only with considerable effort.

@Z: Card Fees at Restaurants

<pic images/bill-cc-fee.ps This restaurant adds 3% to the bill for card payments.>

<em>Michael</em>
Credit-card companies used to strictly prohibit merchants from charging higher
prices for card payments. If a store broke this rule, the card company
terminated its merchant agreement.

<pic images/irs-cc.ps Paying back taxes by credit card incurs fees.>

Over the years, however, this once ironclad rule has softened like a used
diaper. About 15 years ago, gas stations began charging different prices for
cash and card payments. A few years ago, I sat in a restaurant and rubbed my
eyes in amazement for the first time because the establishment charged an extra
3% unless you paid in cash. And recently, at a liquor store in Florida, I had

@Z: Rent Increase

<pic images/rent-control.ps Rent control in San Francisco: Rent Control>

<em>Angelika</em> As we had feared, a rent increase notice landed in our
mailbox at the end of July. As you diligent newsletter readers know, our
building was sold last year. Since our building is subject to San Francisco's
rent control regulations, the rent may only be increased once a year by a
specified percentage. This year, the city set it at 1.6%. The landlord must
give at least 30 days' notice of a rent increase. So we will have to pay the
adjusted rent starting September 1. We knew from the outset that this would
happen, as all our neighbors had already received rent increases. Our previous
landlord, Gus, had not raised the rent every year, and his daughter, who later
took over management, did not get around to it until she wanted to sell the
building.

Rents in San Francisco have been exorbitantly high for as long as anyone can
remember. The city is currently experiencing another full-blown boom, driven
by artificial intelligence companies that are springing up like mushrooms all
over San Francisco. It reminds us very much of the dot-com boom. Average rents
are currently around $2,700 to $2,800 a month; depending on size and
neighborhood, one-bedroom apartments range from $3,500 to $4,100, while a
two-bedroom apartment will set you back between $4,900 and $6,000 a month.
Absolutely insane! Since the neighborhood where we live is highly desirable,
prices here tend to be at the upper end of the range. Another problem is that
there are hardly any apartments available at all. The vacancy rate has once
again become extremely low and, depending on the source, is around 2–4%.
That is why our apartment is still exceptionally inexpensive by
San Francisco standards: even with garage spaces and despite the rent
increase, we are still paying below market rate.

<pic images/rent-increase.ps Oh no, the landlord is raising the rent!>

Interestingly, the rent increase notice also listed several rather opaque
charges. Together, they amount to only a little over $5 a month, but you have
to keep your wits about you to avoid being taken for a ride. One charge was
labeled "Water Bond Passthrough 2025," and the other "Bond Measure Passthrough
2025–26." These are costs that, under certain conditions, the landlord can
pass on to the tenants in addition to the rent. In the case of the first
charge, San Francisco allows landlords to pass 50% of certain water-rate
increases on to tenants. However, this fee may not become part of the base
rent.

In addition, the city allows bond measure costs to be passed through for
rent-controlled apartments in San Francisco ("Bond Measure Passthrough"). This
means that landlords may pass a certain portion of property-tax increases
arising from voter-approved general obligation bonds directly on to their
tenants as a separate monthly surcharge. And people say the US does not have
its share of bureaucracy!

Amusingly, we are now also receiving interest on our security deposit for the
first time. Apparently, this interest must now be paid out annually in San
Francisco. That had never happened before. Here, too, the city sets the
interest rate anew each year. It is currently 4.2%. The landlord may then
deduct 50% of that as an administrative fee for the San Francisco Rent Board.
Who comes up with these things?

@Z: Yuengling Beer

<pic images/yuengling.ps The surprisingly good Yuengling lager.>

<em>Michael</em> About 40 years ago, America had nothing but insipid beer, but
for around 20 years now, every supermarket has stocked specialty brews that
sometimes relegate German brewers to second place. Nothing new there! But every
now and then, we travel through previously unexplored territory—such as rural
northwestern Florida recently—and the selection thins out noticeably.

While searching the supermarket refrigerator, I noticed the old-fashioned
lettering on the label of a lager I had sampled some time ago during a
conference in Pennsylvania and awarded my "surprisingly good" seal of approval.
It is the lager made by
<LINK>https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuengling|Yuengling</LINK>
(pronounced "yingling") of Pottsville, Pennsylvania, reputedly "America's
oldest brewery" (197 years old).

At 4.5% alcohol, the brew does not hit as hard as the generally more potent hop
bombs known as IPAs. The amber-colored "Yuengling Traditional Lager" is
finely balanced and full-bodied, and its flavor almost reminds me a little of
Augustiner Edelstoff. Yuengling produced this type of beer even before
Prohibition in the United States (1920–1933) and did not bring it back to the
market until 1987.

The Yuengling brewery was founded in 1829 by an immigrant from Swabia named
David Gottlieb Jüngling. Since no American can pronounce the umlaut "ü"
correctly, he renamed the company "Yuengling," which Americans pronounce
similarly, if we are being very generous. An outstanding beer—when nothing
else is available!

@Z: Invented in San Francisco: The Mission Burrito

<pic images/burrito-plate.ps Mission burrito, chips, and salsa dip>

<em>Michael</em> Our Noe Valley neighborhood borders the Mexican Mission
District, where naturally there is delicious Mexican food in abundance. I
remember that years before we moved, we once ate at a Mexican restaurant in
Munich. It was good, but expensive. Here, Mexican food is inexpensive fast
food. One example is the so-called "burrito," a forearm-length roll made from
a wafer-thin flour tortilla (or "wrap," in modern parlance) and filled with
all kinds of ingredients such as meat, rice, lettuce, and sauces.

Just as the döner kebab in Germany is not an authentically Turkish dish but a
foreign adaptation created by Turkish immigrants for the German palate, the
Mission burrito is an American invention derived from a similar Mexican dish
but enhanced with rice, guacamole, and sour cream. Where was the burrito
invented? In San Francisco! Either at "Taqueria La Cumbre" or at "El Faro"—the
experts disagree. Both establishments, however, are in the Mission District.

<pic images/burrito-counter.ps Employees assemble burritos to order at the counter.>

It is also important to know that you do not simply enter a taqueria and say,
"One burrito with everything," as you might with a döner kebab (although,
according to <LINK>https://www.youtube.com/shorts/2mO_tAkI5BU|YouTube|No More Döner with Everything</LINK>,
you apparently no longer do that either). Instead, you move along the counter
with the server and select from the different ingredients. The beans alone are
available as pinto, black, or refried. There is usually orange-colored seasoned
rice as well, though it is not mandatory, and for the protein the customer can
choose among beef (carne asada), pork (pastor), and chicken (pollo). These main
categories are divided into further subcategories. The beef, for example,
usually comes in small steak cubes that are quickly seared. If it has to be
chicken, I like to order it with
<LINK>https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_(sauce)|"Mole"|mole sauce</LINK>, a thick,
chocolate-brown sauce that is hard to describe and very, very delicious. All
told, a typical taqueria offers around 20 kinds of meat filling.

<pic images/burrito-front.ps The Mexican takeout place around the corner from us.>

With a regular burrito, that is where the choices end. The slightly more
expensive super burrito also includes guacamole (mashed avocado), sour cream,
and salsa—typically
<LINK>https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pico_de_gallo|pico de gallo</LINK>
or a puréed version spiced with chili peppers. You can also add lettuce, chili
peppers, or herbs such as cilantro.

About once a week, I go to "Casa Mexicana" around the corner and have them wrap
up a burrito to go. Incidentally, many Mexican day laborers who are currently
working on various houses in the neighborhood eat there too. At home, I eat
half of the enormous burrito and save the rest for Angelika, who has it for
lunch at school the next day. As for the cost: in 1997, the super burrito at
the place around the corner cost six dollars. Today it is $16. Inflation! I
have heard from several people who moved away from San Francisco that one of
the things they miss most is the delicious burritos from the Mission. The dish
is still reasonably common in the Bay Area, but in other states you often
search for it in vain.

Greetings from the city of tasty treats:

Angelika and Michael
