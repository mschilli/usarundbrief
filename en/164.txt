@Z: Electoral Defeat for Trump

<pic images/election-chavez.ps We got to vote again in San Francisco>

<em>Angelika</em> Unless you live on a deserted island without internet or electricity, you already know that we had an election here on November 3. I mentioned it in the last newsletter. The so-called midterms were coming up. We also elected a new governor in California, an office roughly equivalent to a state premier in the German system. And, as usual, the ballot (Figure (#6)) also included local elections, such as the city supervisor for our district, several judges, California's attorney general, the city treasurer, the school board, and two dozen ballot measures. Altogether, we had to mark 34 (!) different choices.

<pic images/ballot.ps (#6) The first page of our four-page ballot.>

But first, the federal elections: Traditionally, the sitting president's party loses seats in the midterms, especially in the House of Representatives. Forecasts before the election therefore suggested that the Democrats could make substantial gains there and even win a majority in the House.

Over the years, however, pollsters have become somewhat more cautious. You may remember 2016, when Hillary Clinton was already being treated as the sure winner, only for everything to turn out quite differently and Trump to move into the White House. Back then, pollsters were tearing their hair out and had to thoroughly rethink their forecasting models. This time, they therefore considered it possible, but rather unlikely, that the Democrats would also win a majority in the Senate.

<pic images/election-closed.ps The public ballot drop box is now closed.>

That has mainly to do with the unusual electoral system: Every state, regardless of its size, is represented by two senators. In addition, only about a third of the total of 100 seats come up for election every two years. That makes it quite difficult to wrest a seat from the opposing party and change the balance of power in this chamber.

But as you now know, the blue wave really did sweep through. Some particularly ambitious journalists even called it a blue tsunami. In the United States, blue stands for the Democrats and red for the Republicans. Voters were simply fed up and rebelled at the ballot box. In several traditionally Republican states, the Democrats unexpectedly won important Senate seats, including in Texas, Kansas, Iowa, Ohio, North Carolina, South Carolina, Maine, and Mississippi. Kansas is thus sending a Democratic senator to Washington for the first time since 1932: Adam Hamilton, a Methodist pastor. So the Democrats not only won a majority in the House of Representatives but also took back the Senate. That gives them a majority in both chambers of Congress for the next two years.

<pic images/election-house.ps Distribution of seats in the House of Representatives after the November 3, 2026 election>

The election was quite clearly a protest vote against Trump's policies and the many promises that, in the eyes of many voters, had gone unfulfilled. Persistently high living costs were repeatedly cited as a source of dissatisfaction. Inflation, tariffs, and, not least, the war with Iran, which has had a particularly noticeable effect on gasoline prices here, have taken a financial toll on many people. Farmers in the country's heartland, usually loyal Republican voters, also voted Democratic because they were suffering particularly badly from tariffs and high diesel prices for their farm machinery. On top of that came growing criticism of the numerous allegations of corruption and conflicts of interest surrounding the man in the White House. That really got voters riled up!

Another important factor was Trump's aggressive immigration policy. Many voters felt that the increasingly harsh deportations and operations by the immigration agency ICE had gone too far. The Republican Party felt the effects particularly among voters with Latino backgrounds. Many of them had voted for Trump in 2024 because they hoped he would improve the economy and bring immigration under control. Now quite a few turned back to the Democrats. In Texas, for example, around 60% of Latino voters chose the Democratic candidate <LINK>https://www.dallasnews.com/news/politics/article/latino-voters-swing-left-in-midterm-elections-22516101.php|James Talarico</LINK>. His Republican opponent, Ken Paxton, was also thoroughly mired in scandal, though.

<pic images/election-senate.ps Distribution of seats in the Senate after the November 3, 2026 election>

A protest vote is always somewhat problematic, because you are voting against something rather than necessarily making a deliberate choice for something else. According to a recent poll, the Democratic Party is still not particularly popular despite its election victory. Only 39% of respondents expressed a favorable view of the Democrats; for the Republicans, <LINK>https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2026/05/01/americans-continue-to-view-both-the-republican-and-democratic-parties-negatively/|the figure was 40%</LINK>. Voters now want to see changes. I just hope the newly elected Democrats do not get sidetracked and instead focus their energy on the truly important issues. The new majority does not mean that Trump has now been stripped of his political power, however. The president remains in office and can continue pursuing his own policies, particularly through his extensive veto power. But the Democrats now have considerably more opportunities to scrutinize his administration, block legislation, and establish investigative committees. Things are therefore likely to become more uncomfortable for Trump politically.

@Z: The Atomic Museum in Las Vegas

<pic images/lvam-entrance.ps Your roving newsletter reporter outside the Atomic Museum in Las Vegas.>

<em>Michael</em> In early September, we spent a long weekend in Las Vegas. That may sound crazy to you, because Las Vegas has become quite a tourist trap, at least around the so-called "Strip." But since we met there many years ago, Las Vegas has a special meaning for us, and we drop by from time to time. As old hands, we naturally know the place like the backs of our hands, book hotels nobody else books, and shop at ordinary grocery stores like the locals. For meals, we head to the Arts District or downtown, far from the Strip and its casinos. Off the beaten track, prices in Las Vegas are roughly comparable to San Francisco, and you actually get excellent food and service for your money.

<pic images/lv-video-poker.ps The newsletter editorial team playing casino machines>

Las Vegas also has some offbeat museums. We have already visited the "Mob Museum" (gangsters such as Al Capone and FBI bigwigs such as J. Edgar Hoover) and the "Neon Museum" (<RBLINK>20191201#3</RBLINK>) with its huge old neon signs. This time I had the brilliant idea of visiting the "Atomic Museum." The admission price of $28 per person was pretty steep, but at least I, as a longtime technology buff, got my money's worth.

<pic images/lvam-little-boy.ps Model of the first atomic bomb, "Little Boy," which was dropped on Hiroshima.>

After an introduction to the basics of nuclear fission, the exhibit moved on to the details of the Manhattan Project. As we recall: During World War II, Germany and Japan attacked the rest of the world and were almost unstoppable, and the Americans launched a research project to build a new type of bomb with devastating destructive power. The result was the atomic bomb "Little Boy," which the Americans dropped on the Japanese city of Hiroshima (<RBLINK>20020602#29</RBLINK>). The consequences were disastrous: 100,000 dead and a completely destroyed city. Something similar happened with the second bomb ("Fat Man") over Nagasaki three days later, and the Japanese surrendered.

<pic images/lvam-fat-man.ps The second atomic bomb, "Fat Man," was dropped on Nagasaki and forced Japan to surrender.>

The museum devotes little attention to the entirely legitimate question of how far this concentrated destruction was morally justified. The exhibition clearly focuses on technical aspects such as bomb design, further development, and testing procedures. And, of course, America has a different view of World War II than, for example, Japan, as we learned many years ago during a visit to a museum in Hiroshima. There, the story was that Japan had been dragged into the war by adverse circumstances. Well, opinions differ on that!

<pic images/lvam-office.ps A recreated office of an atomic bomb researcher on the Manhattan Project.>

In any case, I can recommend the museum to any technology enthusiast. In the museum's own theater, you can experience a simulated atomic bomb explosion, complete with booming speakers and shaking benches. You should bring some background knowledge and an appetite for technology; otherwise, it might not be all that interesting.

@Z: Billionaire Tax and Local Squabbling

<pic images/prop40-illu.ps Proposition 40 sought to introduce a wealth tax on billionaires.>

<em>Angelika</em> On November 3, we also voted on some important California candidates and measures. Not least, as already mentioned, we elected a new governor. Normally, that would have been big news, but after the primaries, the campaign was positively uneventful. Xavier Becerra, who decisively defeated Republican Steve Hilton by about 66 to 34 percent, had apparently chosen a strategy of giving his opponents as little ammunition as possible. For example, he held only a single election debate with Hilton. His strategy seems to have worked.

The "Billionaire Tax," a wealth tax on California billionaires (for the distinction between the English "billionaire" and the German "Milliardär," see <RBLINK>20170820#6</RBLINK>), on the other hand, was on everyone's lips, including in the international press. California voters were to decide on it through Proposition 40, a ballot initiative.

<pic images/larry-sf.ps Oracle billionaire Larry Ellison has already sold his house in San Francisco's upscale Pacific Heights neighborhood.>

California is known for its progressive politics and for Silicon Valley, which has produced not only some groundbreaking innovations but also more than a few billionaires. When I asked my students a few years ago what they wanted to be when they grew up, many answered, "Like Elon Musk!" These days, that is rather frowned upon. In general, Americans were not particularly envious of the wealthy for a long time. After all, the notion that you could easily go from rags to riches in America was remarkably persistent. The idea behind it: Anyone can make it, and who knows, perhaps one day you will be one of them yourself. That attitude has changed considerably in recent years, however.

<pic images/pissed-off-voters-guide.ps The radical left campaigns for expropriating the super-rich.>

Proposition 40 proposed a one-time tax of five percent on the taxable net worth of California billionaires. The tax would apply to their property rather than their income: stocks, stakes in companies, artworks, and other assets, for example. Real estate and certain pension and retirement accounts were exempt. Proposition 40 thus proposed a one-time tax of five percent on the wealth of billionaires residing in California. It would have affected around 200 to 250 super-rich people. Now, I certainly support fairer taxation and tax laws with fewer loopholes. But unfortunately, nobody seems to have the patience for fundamental, well-considered reforms these days, politicians included.

<pic images/gary-tan-billionaire-tax.ps Tech mogul Garry Tan explains the negative effects of the proposed billionaire tax.>

One example of the absurdity of the American tax system is the ability to pledge stocks or other company holdings as collateral for a loan. No taxes are due on the loan proceeds because, under tax law, they are borrowed money rather than income. At the same time, the stocks are not sold, so no capital gains tax is due on the gains accumulated up to that point but not yet realized. Only the interest on the loan has to be paid. In the United States, this financing strategy is known as "Buy, Borrow, Die." If the loan remains outstanding until the owner's death, the heirs can benefit from a special provision in U.S. tax law: the so-called "step-up in basis." This generally raises the tax basis of the inherited stocks to their current market value at the time of death. The heirs can then sell some of the stocks to repay the loan without having to pay capital gains tax on the appreciation during the original owner's lifetime. Under certain conditions, gains accumulated over decades can thus remain permanently exempt from capital gains tax.

But back to Proposition 40: In my opinion, the proposed one-time tax was so poorly thought out and cobbled together that you could almost imagine the entire plan had been scribbled on a beer coaster in a bar after a night of heavy drinking. Initially, the money was primarily intended to offset substantial cuts to health care. What was behind those cuts? Obamacare, the health care law introduced under President Obama, had allowed states to expand their public health insurance programs to cover significantly more low-income people. In California, this program is called Medi-Cal. It is funded jointly by the state and the federal government.

<pic images/prop40-sf.ps If San Francisco had had its way, the billionaire tax would actually have passed.>

With the federal law passed in July 2025, the so-called One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the Trump administration enacted substantial cuts to Medicaid, known in California as Medi-Cal. These include funding restrictions and new eligibility requirements. The effects will be felt over several years and place a considerable strain on California's budget. The "Billionaire Tax" was intended to make up for some of this lost federal funding. Ninety percent of the revenue was to go to health care, particularly Medi-Cal. The remaining ten percent was earmarked for education and food assistance. In my opinion, it is always a bad idea simply to plug a budget hole temporarily without coming up with a sustainable solution. After all, the cuts are not limited to a single year.

The "Billionaire Tax" was also backed by an extremely controversial figure: Dave Regan, president of the health care union SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West. His union had played a leading role in developing and promoting the tax initiative. Dave Regan is controversial mainly because he is accused of using his union's power and political campaigns as leverage to advance his own union's interests. According to those allegations, Regan offered Governor Newsom to withdraw the Billionaire Tax from the ballot in exchange for concessions benefiting his union. These supposedly included union contracts with certain hospitals and clinics. Shades of the Mafia.

Our still-serving governor, Gavin Newsom, had in fact spoken out against the "Billionaire Tax." He feared the tax would do California's economy more harm than good, partly by prompting some of the affected billionaires to relocate their homes and businesses. After all, as we know, there is no wall around California yet, and everyone is free to come and go, including moving to another state. Google co-founder Sergey Brin, for example, simply moved his residence to Nevada. It was also very unclear how the value of the assets was supposed to be determined in the first place. Nobody had really thought that through. In the end, though, California voters surprisingly voted against Proposition 40. Around 53 percent rejected the tax, while 47 percent supported it.

<pic images/prop-42.ps The counter-initiative to Prop 40: Prop 42>

On top of that, opponents of the Billionaire Tax had put their own initiative on the ballot, Proposition 42. It was intended to make it harder to introduce new taxes on certain personal assets as well as retroactive taxes. Proposition 42 passed, while Proposition 40 failed. The wealth tax was thus not only rejected in the vote on Proposition 40; an additional obstacle to similar tax plans was created at the same time.

In general, though, I have to say that I am growing tired of all this voting on propositions. The ballots keep getting longer, and some of these initiatives are worded in such complicated, convoluted language that even a person of perfectly normal intelligence can barely understand them after reading them several times. Sometimes I wonder whether this really is still the much-praised direct democracy, or whether someone simply wants to annoy voters.

<pic images/prop-g.ps Another vote on closing the Great Highway to cars: It is now called "Sunset Dunes Park," and reopening it would supposedly cost 20 million.>

A particularly fine example is the Great Highway along Ocean Beach in San Francisco. We have now had to vote on the future of this road for the third time in four years. First it was about temporarily closing it to cars, then permanently turning it into a park, and now partially reopening it to cars during the week. On November 3, Proposition G, which would have reopened it on weekdays, was once again rejected. Around two-thirds of voters voted against it. But at some point, enough is enough. Perhaps it is time simply to accept a decision.

@Z: Turkey Is Now Called Turkey-Yay?

<pic images/tv-turkey-yay.ps Sports commentators no longer call Turkey "Turkey," but "Turkey-Yay">

<em>Michael</em> Before 2026 draws to a close, I would like to add my own two cents about last summer's soccer World Cup. Anyone with ears to hear (or eyes to read) already knows that although I play soccer on an amateur team myself, I simply cannot understand why anyone would waste their time watching 22 fools kick a ball around on television. And then there is all the constant cheating in professional soccer: sneaky fouls, fake injuries, conning the referee into awarding penalties. I just find it stupid and embarrassing.

Accordingly, I followed last summer's World Cup with a fairly casual attitude, always laughing out loud whenever another player spouted political nonsense or otherwise landed in the gossip columns. And one other oddity caught my attention this time: Whenever American television sports commentators mentioned Turkey's soccer team, they no longer said "Turkey," but "Turkey-Yay."

Now I need to back up a little: In English, "Turkey" is not only the name of the country but also the name of the bird. It has been that way for centuries, and every possible joke combining the country and the poultry has long since been exhausted. So why the sudden name change?

<pic images/wild-turkey.ps You sometimes see a "wild turkey" on hiking trails in California.>

Curious, I looked it up online and discovered that, by official order of its head of state, Erdogan, Turkey no longer wants to be called "Turkey" in English, but <LINK>https://en.as.com/soccer/world-cup/why-turkey-is-called-turkiye-at-the-2026-fifa-world-cup-f202606-n-3/|"Turki-ye"</LINK>. American sports commentators are happy to submit to politically correct dogma and eagerly embraced this, but they pronounced the new name "Turkey-Yay!" In American English, "yay!" normally expresses a burst of enthusiasm, like the German "Juhu!" It did not help, of course: Like quite a few other teams, "Turkey-Yay!" was knocked out of the competition early.

But let's take this a step further: Can a country simply change its name in a foreign language? Will Hungary turn up at the next World Cup with a name change because there are countless jokes about its English name, "Hungary," which sounds practically the same as "hungry" in American English? Where will it all end?

@Z: Robot Replaces Coffee Shop Barista

<pic images/robo-coffee.ps There is a robot at SFO airport that makes coffee.>

<em>Michael</em>In America, the low-wage sector has disappeared in recent years. Here in California, the state-mandated minimum wage is currently $16.90, and in San Francisco it is as high as $19.61. That is good news for employees, who no longer have to work for five dollars an hour, but the side effect is that products involving service have become much more expensive.

<pic images/sandwiches-airport.ps (#7) These sandwiches at the airport are so expensive that the shop owner hides the prices.>

For example, an espresso drink at a coffee shop here currently costs six dollars and up (in 2026), and over-the-top creations will probably soon break the ten-dollar barrier. Customers groan, and restaurant operators, no fools themselves, have recently stopped putting the full price on their menus, instead tacking absurd fees onto the bill at checkout.

<pic images/employee-surcharge.ps (#8) The price tags on the merchandise in this shop do not show the selling price, which is 5% higher.>

Of course, Americans have always been used to paying more than the advertised price in the end. Businesses do not include sales tax in their prices, but add it to the bill later at checkout (<RBLINK>20111114#2</RBLINK>). And it does not stop there: Many businesses increasingly add the now-customary 20% restaurant tip to the bill automatically, leaving customers only the option of tipping *more* than 20% (<RBLINK>20210726#8</RBLINK>).

<pic images/no-price-tags.ps These baked goods are also so expensive that the shop does not display any prices.>

Then there are the recent made-up surcharges, such as an "Employee Surcharge," which is extracted from the surprised customer for no apparent reason (for example, 5% in Figure (#8)). This probably originated with restaurants passing their employees' health insurance costs on to customers (<RBLINK>20080502#4</RBLINK>), but has since become completely arbitrary. Many businesses now also pass card-processing fees on to customers who pay by card (<RBLINK>20260810#8</RBLINK>).

<pic images/napali-fee.ps Napali Brewing Co on Kauai arbitrarily adds 3% to every bill, whether you pay cash or by card.>

Some goods have also become so expensive that certain businesses no longer put price tags on them at all, leaving customers to pray that a reasonable amount will pop up at the register. I wonder how much the sandwiches in Figure (#7) cost? Certainly more than ten dollars, or the price would be displayed. Now, I am no fan of government intervention in a functioning market, but the authorities ought to put a stop to this rip-off. In Germany, shops have to quote prices including value-added tax, too, rather than playing tricks at the register later.

<GIF>images/coffee-robot-1.gif|The robot barista takes an order and gets to work.</GIF>

In any case, all this fee-grubbing and tip-begging really gets on my nerves. Whenever possible, I avoid businesses that pull these tricks. Recently at SFO airport, I discovered that a coffee stand once again wanted an extra 5% (Figure (#8)), but a few yards farther along there was a futuristic-looking booth where the coffee was made by a robot instead of a barista. After I entered my order on the kiosk's iPad and paid by credit card (without a fee), an industrial robot behind a thick pane of glass sprang into action. It picked up two paper cups in succession and placed each under a coffee machine, which then slowly dripped a latte macchiato and a cappuccino into them.

<GIF>images/coffee-robot-2.gif|The robot barista places the finished coffee drinks in the pickup compartment.</GIF>

The coffee was about as good as what you get from a fully automatic machine, so it was no match for a professionally prepared espresso drink. But okay, it is probably only a matter of time before the robot masters those simple movements, too. The robot arm then grabbed the filled coffee cups and placed them in the pickup compartment. After I tapped the name I had entered earlier on another iPad, a glass panel slid aside and I could collect the two coffees. The cups cost seven dollars each, roughly in line with the outrageous prices unfortunately customary at the airport. Naturally, the robot demanded neither employee surcharges nor health insurance contributions, and it did not beg for a tip either.

<pic images/kauai-recovers.ps Kauai has to get back on its feet after Hurricane Lowell.>

<em>Angelika</em>
We love vacationing on Hawaii. We step off the plane
there and feel completely relaxed almost immediately. The sweet scent of the
tropics, the gentle breeze, and the friendly people work their magic.
Regular readers of our newsletter know that we take every opportunity to visit
the islands. My fall break in October was to be our next opportunity.
I had been eagerly counting down to the break for weeks, and we were looking
forward to our weeklong vacation on Kauai.

<pic images/kauai-poipu.ps The famous Poipu Beach was hit hard.>

But then came a nasty shock: About
four weeks before our departure, Hurricane Lowell swept across Kauai.
Hurricanes rarely hit Hawaii at full force, but
when they approach the islands, it is no picnic. This year, a
particularly active El Niño year with an unusually warm Pacific, the
Hawaiian Islands were hit by several fierce storms. Back in 1992, Kauai
had already been devastated by a hurricane named Iniki. That storm passed
directly over the island, destroying or damaging much of its
housing and infrastructure.

<pic images/kauai-sand.ps Hawaii residents are asked to dump buckets of sand onto the beach stripped bare by the wind.>

Then, in September 2026, a new hurricane named Lowell passed west of the very
secluded island of Niihau, once again bringing strong winds, heavy
rainfall, and dangerously high waves to Kauai. The southern and western
coasts of the island suffered particularly severe damage, while the north experienced
flooding and road closures.
Princeville, our
vacation destination, and the North Shore, with the lovely town of Hanalei,
where we especially enjoy spending time, also took a severe beating. Gulp,
things did not look good at all.

<pic images/kauai-construction.ps Even four weeks after the hurricane, construction crews are still clearing fallen trees.>

Of course, we immediately began worrying: Should we even go, and would it
be possible? Would power and water be back on? Would the
locals be completely fed up with tourists because we were
getting in the way of cleanup efforts and using already scarce resources? So I
wrote to our Airbnb host. She was confident
that at least in Princeville, most things would be restored
by the time we would arrive.

<pic images/kauai-traffic.ps You often have to wait 15 minutes while construction workers move things around.>

We were also reassured by the news that the airport was already
operating again on generators just one day after the hurricane.
Truly amazing for such a small island! So we decided to give it a try.
After all, Hawaii depends on tourism, and we simply prepared ourselves
for the possibility that not everything would be open yet. You just have to
be flexible.

<pic images/hanalei-lifeguard.ps The hurricane uprooted a massive tree on our favorite beach on Kauai.>

And it was exactly the right decision. When we arrived at the airport,
everything went so smoothly that for a brief moment we forgot the island
had only just begun repairing the hurricane damage. We were
truly astonished at how much had already been accomplished.

<pic images/hanalei-shower.ps Hurricane Lowell left its mark here.>

Unfortunately, the hurricane had badly damaged our favorite restaurant in Hanalei, "Kalypso."
It will probably remain closed for many months because the roof
was severely damaged. That really did make us sad, since we
had spent so many happy hours there, and the place was always buzzing.

<pic images/hanalei-kalypso-compare.ps Our favorite restaurant, "Kalypso" in Hanalei, suffers roof damage in 2026 and remains closed for months.>

Tiki Iniki in Princeville, another place we enjoy visiting, on the other hand,
had already reopened. Not every dish was available yet, but
the food was hearty and as good as ever. The restaurant is also known for its
delicious, strong Mai Tais and belongs to rock star Todd Rundgren, who
played in the bands Nazz and Utopia, and his wife Michele. The waiter who
served us looked like an aging rock star himself.

<pic images/kauai-produce-price.ps Vegetable prices at the supermarket have risen significantly.>

He told us that
they had gone two weeks without power or water and had therefore
had to throw out all the food in their freezers. They were grateful for every
tourist and guest. And did you attentive readers notice anything? That's right:
The restaurant has the same name as the hurricane of 1992. Todd and
Michele ended up on the island shortly afterward.

<pic images/kauai-cleanup-excavator.ps Crews are gathering fallen trees everywhere ...>
<pic images/kauai-cleanup-trucks.ps ... and hauling away the debris in heavy trucks.>

Poipu, at the south end of the island, was also hit hard by Hurricane Lowell. The wind
had blown so much sand onto the parking lot that parts of it were
completely buried. Some inexperienced tourists even got their cars
stuck in the sand. It was all the more astonishing to see people already
happily swimming in the water while, in the background, workers were replacing
broken windows on the nearby houses.

<pic images/poipu-turtles.ps The giant turtles on Poipu Beach seemed unfazed.>

And Poipu's famous turtles? They, too, were already
back on the beach, basking in the sun as though all the commotion
had not bothered them in the slightest. The people of Kauai seemed
to be following their example: Roll up your sleeves, clean up, and carry on.
An impressive calmness and resilience that we could certainly
learn a thing or two from.

@Z: Grocery Outlet

<pic images/go-checkout.ps You sometimes encounter strange characters shopping in the poorer neighborhoods.>

<em>Michael</em> As you know, I am the chief grocery shopper in our household, and I enjoy it, too. With decades of experience in the field, I therefore know exactly which store carries which product, which aisle it is in, and what it normally costs. Because it is certainly not the case that you can drive to one supermarket here and get everything in one stop.

Far from it! The bread at the Safeway supermarket chain, for example, is inedible, and only the organic hippie store Rainbow Grocery carries loaves that suit our German-shaped tastes. Ordinary yogurt that is not overly sweet is available at reasonable prices only at the Aldi clone "Trader Joe's." And for wine, which is notoriously expensive in America, the only real options are the Costco and Grocery Outlet chains. Wine shops charge astronomical prices for overhyped plonk.

<pic images/go-milka.ps (#3) German Milka at Grocery Outlet.>
<pic images/go-milka-back.ps (#4) It really is the German Milka product.>

Grocery Outlet is a sort of bargain-basement clearance store for groceries. Nothing there costs a "normal" price; instead, it costs such-and-such, but "more elsewhere." For instance, the price tag on a plastic bottle containing a quart (a quarter of a gallon, about a liter) of milk reads "$2.99, elsewhere $3.99." I have my doubts about whether that is always quite accurate, but that is the concept. I cannot verify, for example, whether the German Milka chocolate costs "$3.49 elsewhere," as shown in Figure (#3), especially since there is nowhere else in California to buy German Milka, except perhaps at <LINK>https://usarundbrief.com/48/images/lehrs.html|Lehr's</LINK>.

<pic images/go-shoes.ps A customer with interesting footwear.>

I save a fortune this way, particularly in the wine department, because Grocery Outlet buys up remaining stock from well-known Napa Valley wineries that otherwise sell their fine wines at astronomical prices. The latter is very easy to verify: Just drive to Napa Valley, go wine tasting, and tear your hair out over a fairly ordinary bottle of red wine costing $50 at upscale wineries like "Chateau St Jean."

<pic images/go-saved.ps (#1) Who does not like saving 162 dollars on groceries?>

The next day, the customer sees the same bottle at Grocery Outlet for $9.99 ("$50 elsewhere"). We might as well buy two! Or even six, because on Thursdays the Pacifica branch gives you another 10% off (Figure (#2))! So if you ever shop at Grocery Outlet and see some nut scanning all the wines on the shelves with the "Vivino" app and checking their ratings, that is me!

<pic images/go-wine10.ps (#2) Another 10 percent off when you buy six bottles of wine on Thursdays.>

I suspect Grocery Outlet's business model calls for cheap retail rents, because its half-dozen locations within San Francisco are in rather, well, rougher areas. In general, Grocery Outlet branches are never in upscale neighborhoods, but quite the opposite: They are in parts of town ordinary citizens are reluctant to venture into. Cautious tourists should generally avoid them or visit only with a local guide. In some hotspots plagued by homelessness, a security guard stands at the store entrance. The cashiers are down-to-earth across the board and handle even tense situations confidently. Store management is meticulous about ensuring that everyone behaves and nobody simply empties the store, something that does not work at all at other chains, such as <LINK>https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/walgreens-retail-theft-shoplifting-san-francisco-civic-center-caught-on-camera|Walgreens</LINK>. Once, a cashier at Grocery Outlet even asked me whether I wanted to pay with "EBT" (U.S. food stamps for the poor)! Outrageous!

<pic images/go-map.ps Branches of the "Grocery Outlet" chain tend to be in the rougher neighborhoods.>

Different branches also carry different products, some tailored to the population of their particular neighborhood. @Z: Digging with a 20-Ton Excavator

<GIF>images/bagger.gif|Michael takes charge in the operator's cab</GIF>

<em>Michael</em>
Whenever I see a bulldozer or other heavy equipment at a construction site, I often joke that it would be nice if you could slip a construction worker a banknote and spend a few minutes digging to your heart's content. Never in my wildest dreams did I imagine this was actually possible, but somehow I found the recreation park <LINK>https://bagger-park.de/baggern/|"Bagger Park Emsland"</LINK> online, way up in northern Germany, just a few kilometers from the Dutch border. It offers exactly that: digging with heavy equipment, for the little boy in every man. I made a note of it for our summer visit to Germany, and we drove to Emsland as planned.

<pic images/bagger-mini.ps First, practice on a mini excavator.>

You pay 42 euros per person (my brother-in-law also had a go; Angelika was wary of the whole thing) and first practice for ten minutes on a mini excavator for small construction sites, weighing about a ton. Beforehand, I had already downloaded the iPad app "Digger Sim" and practiced digging a little, because I wanted to hit the ground running.

The controls consist of two joystick-like levers: one on the left and one on the right of a seat where you sit comfortably with your hands on the little levers. Move the left lever to the left or right, and the entire excavator, bucket and all, rotates left or right. Pull the stick back, and the elbow of the excavator arm bends, bringing the bucket closer to the cab. Push forward, and the elbow straightens. That takes care of the left joystick! The right joystick is operated with your right hand. Pull it to the left, and the bucket curls inward; move it to the right, and the bucket opens backward. Push it forward, and the entire arm lowers (the elbow stays bent at the same angle); pull it back, and the arm rises. Pretty complicated, but after a while the movements settle into muscle memory.

<pic images/bagger-sim.ps The iPad app "Digger Sim" gives you a feel for controlling the bucket.>

An excavator instructor gave me a brief introduction, then flipped a lever, called out, "The excavator is live now!" and I very cautiously began moving the bucket. I must have looked a little clumsy, because the instructor showed me another trick: To dig a hole, first point the bucket straight down (right lever to the right or left, depending on the bucket's position), then lower the arm (right lever forward) so the bucket plunges vertically into the ground. Then pull the left lever back so the arm's elbow bends and the bucket pushes dirt toward the cab. Now just pull the right lever to the left to curl the bucket closed, and you have a heaping bucketful of soil to work with!

<pic images/bagger-out.ps Michael exuberantly flings dirt out of the excavator bucket>

Once you have managed not to cause any major accidents with the small excavator, you are allowed to move up to the big, 20-ton machine. You can hardly imagine how much fun that is. When you plunge the enormous bucket into the earth, the excavator's diesel engine roars and blows clouds of soot out its rear end. And best of all, the tremendous leverage of the bucket suddenly lifts the front of the excavator off its tracks, raising the tiny human in the cab half a meter into the air!

<pic images/bagger-map.ps Bagger Park Emsland is in Germany's far northwest.>

The cab also has foot pedals for driving the excavator around on its tracks, but we were not allowed to do that for safety reasons; we amateurs might otherwise have driven our excavators into a hole. After ten minutes of digging with the big excavator, we were honestly completely exhausted. You really have to concentrate to keep that mighty arm, easily ten meters long, from flailing around uncontrollably. All in all, enormous fun, and highly recommended if you are ever in the area.

sea levels along the California coast even higher, making

on top of all that.

In some places, the water could no longer drain away quickly enough because
the drainage systems were overwhelmed, and the high water levels in the

backed up from below. Mission Street near us, for

were also flooded when the bay overflowed its banks at high tide.

At Ocean Beach
here in the city and in Pacifica, south of San Francisco, where Michael

and houses near the coast were also flooded, and seawalls

Our mayor, Daniel Lurie, urged residents to
